Loyalty marketing review · Tier A

Keep the Affluent Spenders, test the young Under-engaged Affluent, and size nothing from this file

Mall customer segmentation — 200 loyalty members, annual income and a mall-assigned Spending Score. Which segments marketing should retain or try to convert, and how far this file can be trusted to size them.

SituationThe mall scores its loyalty members from 1 to 100 on spending and records their age, gender and income. In this file of 200 members the average score is about 50 at every income level, and women and men score within 3 points of each other.

ComplicationMarketing wants to know whom to target. The file cannot answer that directly: it records no campaign response and no purchases, and the formula behind the Spending Score is not documented. The usual answer from this dataset, five income-and-spending segments, is then quoted with segment sizes as if they were shares of a real member base.

The members fall into five segments that are real, stable and reproducible with two income and two score thresholds. But the file was generated in pairs, so its segment sizes mean nothing, and no member over 40 scores above 60. Marketing should protect the 39 Affluent Spenders, test conversion first on the under-40 Under-engaged Affluent with a pilot run on the full member base, and size every budget from the real counts the rule produces.

  1. The fileIt was generated two members at a time. All 100 consecutive ID pairs share an income (random order matches at most 9), and at the income extremes the even-ID twin outspends the odd-ID twin in 61 of 63 pairs. Each corner segment is made of one parity only, so the segment sizes come from the generator, not from a mall. The score is spread evenly from 1 to 99: it behaves like a rank, not like an amount spent.
  2. The structureFive segments, and much stronger than chance: silhouette 0.555 against at most 0.44 from data with no segments. A Gaussian mixture and Ward clustering find the same groups (ARI 0.96 and 0.94), and every segment survives resampling (bootstrap Jaccard ≥ 0.97). Income 40k / 70k and score 40 / 60 reproduce the clustering for 97.5% of members.
  3. The driversIncome does not move the average score (r = 0.01 [-0.13, 0.15]), but scores vary 4.2× as much at the income extremes as in the middle. Age is a ceiling: 0 of 78 members over 40 score above 60, where 24 would if age did not matter. Gender makes no measurable difference (d = 0.12 [-0.19, 0.41]).
  4. The targetAll 39 Affluent Spenders are aged 27–40 with a mean score of 82: they are the core to retain. Of the 35 Under-engaged Affluent (mean score 17), only 15 are 40 or under, where high-spending members of the same age and income exist. Proving a lift needs about 1,458 members per arm, so the pilot must run on the full member base.

Recommendation — Head of CRM & Marketing

  1. Run the two-threshold rule on the full loyalty base and publish real segment counts. One query (income ≥ 70k or ≤ 40k; score ≥ 60 or ≤ 40) assigns every member. Use those counts, never this file's 18% / 20% shares, for any budget. Owner: CRM analytics lead, within two weeks.
  2. Protect the Affluent Spenders without discounting them. They already spend. Offer recognition instead of price: early access, a loyalty tier, events. Track their score month by month as the retention measure. Owner: CRM manager, from next quarter.
  3. Pilot conversion on the under-40 Under-engaged Affluent. Randomise members to offer or holdout within the segment, stratified by gender. The primary metric is redemption or incremental spend from transactions, written down before launch. Size each arm from the power table (about 1,458 per arm for a 50% lift on a 5% response) and run 8–12 weeks with no early stopping. Test the over-40s in a separate, cheaper arm. Owner: Head of CRM & Marketing, design approved this month.
  4. Keep the other three segments on low-cost plans. Mainstream is the largest and most uniform group (score SD 7.6 in the middle income band), so one broad message fits it. Grow Young Enthusiasts with affordability guardrails: no credit or instalment push to members on ≤ 40k. Take Budget Conscious out of paid media. Owner: campaign managers, next campaign calendar.
  5. Get the score definition and transaction data before the pilot reads out. Without them, “spending” stays a number the mall assigns. Legal should confirm that age-based campaign selection is allowed under the mall's privacy policy. Owner: loyalty platform team and legal, before pilot launch.
So what
The key exhibit. K-means on income and score finds five segments; the dashed lines at income 40k / 70k and score 40 / 60 put 97.5% of members in the same segment, and the 5 exceptions all sit on a border. So what
The 200 rows are 100 pairs with identical income, sorted by income before IDs were given out. At income ≥ 70k the odd-ID twin scores at most 40 and the even-ID twin at least 63, without exception. The four corner segments were filled one twin at a time, which is why Affluent Spenders (39) and Under-engaged Affluent (35) are nearly the same size, as are Young Enthusiasts (22) and Budget Conscious (23). Use this file for the shape of the segments, never for their size.

Five segments, and much stronger than chance: silhouette 0.555 against at most 0.44 from data with no segments

So what
Silhouette, Davies–Bouldin and the mixture-model BIC all choose five. Calinski–Harabasz makes its largest gain at five (+74) and creeps up to a peak at eight. The grey band is what k-means finds in 200 data sets with no segments (evenly spread points, or real values shuffled against each other). At k = 5 the real data clears the highest of them by more than 0.1. Adding age as a third input makes every k worse (best 0.43), so age describes the segments but does not define them.

Income does not set how much members spend; it sets how much their spending varies

So what
Every income band averages a score of about 50 (r = 0.01). But in the 41–69k band the score's standard deviation is 7.6 points, against 30.2 at ≤ 40k and 33.4 at ≥ 70k, a 4.2× ratio [3.1, 5.9]. Distance correlation (0.25, permutation p = 0.001) confirms the two are strongly dependent even though the linear correlation is zero. Income tells marketing where targeting matters: in the middle band one message fits almost everyone; at either end the same income holds opposite behaviours, so the offer has to be chosen by score.

No member over 40 scores above 60: high spending is an under-40 behaviour in this file

So what
62 of 122 members aged 40 or under score above 60. Of the 78 over 40, none does; age-blind chance would put 24 there (Fisher p = 1e-17; the 95% upper bound on the over-40 rate is 4.7%). The ceiling holds in every income band, and age is unrelated to income (r = -0.01), so it is not income in disguise. The overall correlation (ρ = -0.34) understates it: this is a wall, not a slope.

So what
The under-40 Under-engaged Affluent earn the same as the Affluent Spenders (mean 88k vs 87k) and are the same age (median 34 vs 32). Their high-spending counterpart exists in the data. The 20 over 40 (median age 47) have no high-spending counterpart anywhere in the file. They are not written off: they get a separate, cheaper test, with lower expectations.

A credible pilot needs the full member base: a 50% lift on a 5% response takes about 1,458 members per arm

So what
The 15 members in this file's pilot slice could only detect an offer that lifts a 5% response rate to 66%. Real campaigns move response by a fraction of that. At a 10% baseline a 50% lift needs 681 per arm; at 2% it needs 3,789. If the real segment is smaller than two arms' worth, the pilot runs longer, accepts a larger minimum lift, or pools both affluent age groups with age as a stratification variable.

Fairness. Segments do not differ by gender (χ² p = 0.69, Cramér's V = 0.11), and the rule uses only income and score. The under-40 pilot slice is 5 of 15 female (33%) against 58% in the rest of the file. With 15 members that is within chance (p = 0.10), but a real pilot should randomise within gender and report by gender. Age decides only the order of testing; over-40s are not excluded from offers.

Appendix · Method and limits

Hypotheses that did not survive

“The file is a clean snapshot of a member base.” Rejected. It was generated in income-sorted pairs, and the data review's “no information content” for the ID is wrong. The segment shapes stand; the segment sizes do not.

“Income does not drive spending.” Half right. It does not set the average score, but it sets the spread, which is what tells marketing where targeting matters.

“The Under-engaged Affluent are the biggest conversion upside.” Narrowed. Only the 15 aged 40 or under have high-spending peers of the same age and income in the file. “Convertible” is still a hypothesis until the pilot reads out.

What this analysis does not claim

No response, no causation. The file has no campaign exposure, response or transactions. Segments describe how members score now, not how they would react to an offer. The pilot design (Part IV) is how that question gets answered.

A teaching dataset, not a mall. 200 members, generated in pairs, with an undefined score and income whose meaning (individual or household) is undocumented. The thresholds are in this file's units and should be re-checked if the real score is computed differently.

Cleaning. Nothing was imputed or dropped: the file has no nulls or duplicate members. Two income values (137k) above the Tukey fence were flagged and kept; removing them changes nothing (ARI 0.98). CustomerID and its parity were barred from every feature set. The log is data/processed/_cleaning_log.csv.

Statistics. 11 hypothesis tests were corrected together (Benjamini–Hochberg); 7 survive. Effect sizes carry bootstrap (2,000 resamples), Fisher-z or Wilson confidence intervals. Non-linear dependence is tested by distance correlation with a permutation null. Because rows are paired by construction, p-values describe this file, not a population.

Segmentation. K-means (k = 5, n_init = 10) on standardised income and score. k chosen by silhouette, Davies–Bouldin and mixture-model BIC, and tested against 200 no-structure data sets. Stability: 50 seeds, 200 bootstrap refits, two other algorithms. A single random start lands elsewhere 42% of the time, so n_init must stay ≥ 10. Pilot sizes come from a two-proportion power calculation (two-sided α = 0.05, power 0.80). The segmentation card is models/segmentation_card.json.

Reproducibility. Six numbered notebooks run in order with RANDOM_STATE = 42 throughout, and a restart-and-run-all from the raw CSV is verified. They import no local modules: chart styling and every helper are defined inline. Every number in this report is read from reports/_key_figures.json, written by notebooks 01–05.
