Portfolio strategy review · Tier A

Size the slate for the power law, localise by genre, and screen with the score

Video game sales, 1980–2016 — 16,595 physical releases, 11,493 titles, 31 platforms. What a third-party publisher can learn from 35 years of physical game sales about where value concentrates and how much of it can be foreseen.

SituationBetween 1980 and 2016, 16,595 physical console and PC releases sold 8.9 billion units. The median release sold 170 thousand. One release in ten earned 59% of all units, and the top 1% earned 21%.

ComplicationA third-party publisher commits a game's budget two to three years before it ships, without knowing which releases will hit. The record it plans from looks hostile: units of new releases fell 45% between 2008–10 and 2012–14. The committee's usual levers — pick the right genre, follow the platform holders — have never been measured against what the data can actually separate.

Physical game sales follow a power law that has been the same for 35 years. What a release sells depends far more on who publishes it, and on which platform, than on its genre. A third-party slate should therefore be sized for a ~12% hit rate, localised by genre, and screened with a launch-time score that finds hits 4.0× more often than picking at random — used to screen projects out, not to choose them.

  1. The recordMost of the post-2010 “collapse” is two platforms retiring. DS and Wii account for 85% of the releases lost between 2008–10 and 2012–14. Hit units fell 37% against 56% for the long tail, so the hit rate per release rose, from 9.9% to 14.4%. The 2015–16 releases have had under two years to sell, so their totals are incomplete.
  2. Where value concentratesThe top tenth of releases earns about 59% of units. Above a common 100k-unit floor that share sits near 48% in every era: the apparent rise after 2005 comes from the database listing more small titles, not from bigger hits. Platform holders publish 10% of releases and earn 30% of units. On the same platform, in the same genre, their games have 4.1× the odds of selling a million.
  3. Where taste differsJapan buys 38% of all role-playing units but only 4% of shooter units. Its ranking of genres is stable across every pair of eras (Spearman ρ 0.70–0.94, well above chance). Europe's share of units grew from 11% to 34%, and its ranking of genres is not stable.
  4. What a committee can foreseePublisher identity explains 5.8× as much of the variation in sales as genre does. Tested on 2012–14 releases it never saw, a score built only from facts known at greenlight puts 57% of its top tenth on hits (46% for third-party releases) and only 2.6% of its bottom half. It ranks well but under-predicts the overall hit rate by half, so it should be read as a ranking, not as a probability.

Recommendation — Head of Portfolio Strategy

  1. Plan the slate on the third-party hit rate, not on conviction. About 12% of third-party releases hit in 2012–14 — one in 9. A 90% chance of at least one hit takes about 19 unscreened releases. The portfolio's fixed costs should be covered by the expected number of hits, not by the committee's favourite title. Owner: Head of Portfolio Strategy, at the next annual slate review.
  2. Localise by genre, not by market. Fund Japanese localisation and marketing for role-playing, strategy, puzzle and adventure games, where Japan takes 22–38% of units. Do not fund it for shooters, racing and sports games, where it takes 4%–10%. Plan on Europe being a third of the market for almost every genre. Owner: regional publishing leads, applied to every project in the current pipeline.
  3. Use a launch-time hit score only to screen projects out. Projects in the bottom half of the ranking hit 2.6% of the time. Re-scope or stop them unless the committee writes down why the score is wrong. Never quote the score as a probability. Owner: Head of Portfolio Strategy, with the analytics team, before the next greenlight round.
  4. Stop benchmarking against first-party hits. A platform holder's own release has 4.1× the odds of selling a million even on the same platform and in the same genre (2.3× without Nintendo). Third-party targets set from first-party comparables are set too high. Owner: finance business partner, in the next budget cycle.
  5. Replace this data source before acting on levels. This file ends in 2016 and covers physical retail only. Buy a current sales source that includes digital, then refit and recalibrate the score on it. The patterns above are the hypotheses to re-test; the numbers are not current. Owner: analytics lead, this quarter.
So what
The key exhibit. The score was trained only on releases up to 2011 and tested on 1,698 releases from 2012–14 whose titles it had never seen. Its top tenth hit 57% of the time, against a base rate of 14.4%; its bottom half hit 2.6%. It beats the committee's own rules of thumb: the publisher's track record (0.38 PR-AUC) and the first-party flag (0.25). Four in ten of its top picks still miss, so it supports a decision rather than making one.

Most of the post-2010 slump is two retiring platforms and two censored years — the hit rate per release rose

So what
Releases fell 57% between 2008–10 and 2012–14 (1,383 to 599 a year), and DS and Wii account for 85% of the lost releases. Their successors never drew the same flood of low-budget casual games. Hits fell less than the tail, so the share of releases selling a million rose by 4.3 points [2.5, 6.2]. Leaving out DS and Wii, it moved only from 12.4% to 14.2%. The median 2016 release shows 50k units against 160k for 2014 because it has barely been on sale. No trend line through 2015–16 measures demand.

One release in ten earns 59% of units; the median release sells 170 thousand

So what
The mean release (0.54M units) sells three times the median and is pulled up by a handful of outliers. The 90th percentile sells 1.2M and the 99th 5.4M. Counted by title instead of by game-and-platform, inequality is higher (Gini 0.75 vs 0.71), because the biggest games also ship on the most platforms. A slate planned on the average release is planned on a number almost no release achieves.

Concentration did not rise after 2005: above a common 100k floor, the top tenth's share is flat at about 50% in every era

So what
Across all releases, the top tenth's share rose by 5.0 points after 2005 [1.9, 8.2]. Over the same years the share of releases selling under 100k rose from 13% to 38%. The database was listing more small titles at the bottom. Among releases above 100k, the rise is 2.6 points [-1.1, 6.4], which is not significant. That is the better planning fact: the power law has been constant for 35 years, not a trend that could reverse.

Platform holders publish 10% of releases and earn 30% of units — on every platform family

So what
A platform holder's own release hits 33% of the time, against 10% for third-party releases. The gap is largest on Nintendo hardware (48% vs 7%) but present on all four families. It does not come from platform holders choosing better platforms or genres. Comparing like with like across 217 platform-and-genre groups, the odds ratio is 4.1 [3.6, 4.6], and 2.3 without Nintendo. This is association: the data cannot separate game quality from bundling, timing and shelf placement.

Who publishes a game explains 6× more of its sales than what kind of game it is

So what
Each factor was scored on held-out titles, so a factor with many categories gains nothing from overfitting. Publisher explains 17.8% of the variance in log units, platform 7.9%, genre 3.1% and release year 2.8%. Genre does shift the odds: Platform games hit 22% of the time, Adventure games 3%. But in every genre the 90th-percentile release sells 30–58× the 10th. Choosing the “right genre” is the weakest lever the committee has.

Part III · Where taste differs

Japan's taste is genre-specific and has held for 35 years; Europe's share has tripled

Japan's share of units fell from 33% to about a ninth after 2000; Europe's tripled to 34%

So what
North America stays at 43%–54% in every era. The change is in the rest of the world: Japan went from a third of the market to 12%, and Europe from 11% to 34%. In 10 of 12 genres, Europe's share was higher in 2011–14 than in 2001–05. Europe is no longer a secondary territory for any genre.

Japan buys role-playing games and not shooters, and has done so in every era

So what
Role-playing games draw 38% of their units from Japan and never less than 31% in any era. Shooters draw 3.7% and have been at 2–3% since 2001. Genre alone accounts for a large share of the variation in a release's Japanese share (ε² = 0.11). Japan's ranking of genres correlates at ρ = 0.70–0.94 across every pair of eras, where chance would reach about 0.50. Europe's ranking ranges from ρ = -0.16 to 0.83. The Japan rule is safe to plan on; the Europe rule is “it is a third of everything”.

No feature family predicts hits on its own: the score works by combining who publishes, on what, and how widely

So what
Used alone, each family of features reaches at most 0.31 PR-AUC; together they reach 0.51. Platform and genre are the weakest family alone (0.21) yet cost the most when removed (0.40). They give context: a publisher's track record means something different on a fading handheld than on a new console. No single rule, such as “only greenlight proven publishers” or “only make shooters”, captures this. That is the case for a model, and the reason it must be refitted on current data before use.

Read it as a ranking, not a probability. The score expects a 7.4% hit rate for a test window that delivered 14.4%. It learned from 2009–11 that hit rates were falling, and then the casual DS/Wii tier disappeared (Part I). The ranking held (PR-AUC 0.51, Brier skill 18%); the level did not. This is the kind of drift a deployed version would have to be recalibrated against every year.

On third-party releases only (1,544 test releases, base rate 11.7%), the top tenth hits 46%. That is the same 4.0× lift as on all releases, so the score is not simply detecting platform holders. For slate sizing, a 90% chance of at least one hit takes about 19 unscreened third-party releases, and about 4 drawn from the score's top tenth. That is an upper bound: a committee ranks its own shorter, already-filtered pipeline.

Appendix · Method and limits

What this analysis does not claim

Units, not money, and physical retail only. The file has no price, revenue, digital, mobile or free-to-play sales. vgchartz figures are modelled estimates, not audited sales, and include copies bundled with consoles (Wii Sports, 82.7M). Every claim describes this record of the market.

It ends in 2016. The patterns most likely to carry over are the power law, the first-party premium and the shape of regional taste. The levels — hit rates, regional shares, the score itself — are ten years old and must be re-measured on a current source before anyone acts on them (recommendation 5).

Nothing here is causal. There is no treatment, no assignment mechanism and no counterfactual. “First-party games hit more” and “publisher identity explains variance” are associations; quality, marketing budget, bundling and release timing are all missing from the file.

Cleaning. 3 rows were removed: one exact duplicate, one scraper stray, and one split record merged into its parent (it carried 0.48M of EU units the main row lacked). 8 impossible years were set to missing. 117 missing years were taken from the same title on another platform, and only where those platforms' years agreed within one year and did not predate the platform. 57 missing publishers were filled the same way. Every imputed value is flagged, and the full log is data/processed/_cleaning_log.csv.

Statistics. 21 hypothesis tests were corrected together (Benjamini–Hochberg); 14 survive. Effect sizes come with confidence intervals from 2,000 bootstrap resamples, or from a permutation null where the sample is small (12-genre rank correlations, 10,000 permutations). The first-party comparison holds platform and genre fixed (Mantel–Haenszel), and the variance comparison uses held-out titles so that publisher's 575 categories cannot win by overfitting.

The model. Gradient-boosted trees ({"learning_rate": 0.06, "max_leaf_nodes": 31, "l2_regularization": 1.0, "max_iter": 400}), with hyperparameters chosen on a 2009–11 validation window. It was trained on 13,680 releases up to 2011 and tested once on 2012–14, with 99 test rows removed because their title was already in training. Releases from 2015–16 were excluded because their sales are incomplete. Features are limited to what is known at greenlight. The publisher's track record uses earlier years only, and that is checked in code. Regional sales, Rank, lifetime platform count and title totals are excluded as leakage. Confidence intervals come from bootstrap resamples by title. The model card is models/model_card.json.

Reproducibility. Six numbered notebooks run in order with RANDOM_STATE = 42 throughout, and a restart-and-run-all from the raw CSV is verified. They import no local modules: chart styling and every helper are defined inline. Every number in this report is read from reports/_key_figures.json, written by notebooks 01–05.
